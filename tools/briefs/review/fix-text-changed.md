1. <p class="honour">This site is built in honour of Edward N. Luttwak. It is not his work and was built without his involvement; it is an attempt to look at the whole world, every state at once, with the lamp he built.

2. <p>Luttwak's central claim (<i>Strategy: The Logic of War and Peace</i>, 1987) is that strategy is paradoxical, because the other side reacts; the argument is set out under The work, below. Every card in this atlas therefore ends with <b>paradoxes</b>: the places where an actor's own instruments undermine its own goals. Serbia withholds its alignment with Brussels to keep UN cover on Kosovo, which rests on the Russian and Chinese veto, and the Commission ties the pace of its negotiations to normalisation with Kosovo. Kosovo's assertion of sovereignty in the north cost it three years of Brussels.

3. <p>Luttwak stacks conflict in levels (set out under The work, below), and above them all sits <b>grand strategy</b>, where military force meets diplomacy, economics, alliances and persuasion, and where a state's whole external conduct can be read as one design. This atlas lives on the top level only. It does not model battles or armies; it models what states and state-like actors want, the means they use across the four domains (diplomatic, informational, military, economic), what constrains them, and how they see each other.

4. <p>Each stance carries a posture (today) and a bond (what would survive a change of government). A <b>feud</b> is bond kin with posture rival, adversary or claimed territory; a <b>Byzantine alliance</b> is bond grievance with posture ally or reluctant ally. A fault written by both sides of a pair is <b>mutual</b>; written by one side only, it says who feels it. The tendency reads the trend against the kind: a warming feud is <b>healing</b>, a cooling one <b>deepening</b>; a cooling Byzantine alliance is <b>tearing</b>. Only hand-written stances of confidence A or B count. Kin graded reluctant ally or reluctant partner is not a fault but a warning, <b>strained</b>, shown only in the ledger under the map. ${shown.length} faults so far, from ${fullActors.length} cards.

5. <p class="lede">A fault is a relationship whose deep level contradicts its surface: the bond says one thing, the posture another. Luttwak's rule is that strategy works only when its levels are in harmony; a relationship resting on a fault is the one that breaks first. Two kinds matter. A <b>feud</b> is kin graded rival, adversary or claimed territory, the quarrel inside the family, the most intense because closeness multiplies the points of contact. A <b>Byzantine alliance</b> is an open wound graded ally: the Byzantine maxim that every enemy is a possible ally, and the alliance that holds over the wound because it must.

6. <p>A <b>fault</b> is a relationship whose deep level contradicts its surface. The frame is Luttwak's structure of <b>levels</b>: in <i>Strategy</i> success at one level can be undone at a higher one, and a strategy works only when its levels are in harmony. Here the levels are two: the bond, which would survive a change of government, and the posture of today. Two contradictions matter. The <b>feud</b> is kin graded rival, adversary or claimed territory, the quarrel inside the family, which Luttwak counts as the most intense of rivalries because closeness multiplies the points of contact. The <b>Byzantine alliance</b> is a grievance graded ally: the maxim from <i>The Grand Strategy of the Byzantine Empire</i> that every enemy is a possible ally, and the alliance that holds over an open wound because it must. The Faults page lists both, says whether each is felt by one side or both, and reads its trend as healing or tearing; on the map a fault shows as a bond band that contradicts the colour of its outline.

7. <p>A <b>seam</b> is an actor pulled two ways: an ally of two actors that are rivals or adversaries of each other. Greece between Israel and Turkey, the United States between Saudi Arabia and the Emirates, Hungary between Croatia and Serbia. The word is Luttwak's. In <i>Strategy</i> he describes <b>relational manoeuvre</b> as the style that aims not at the enemy's strength but at his specific weaknesses, his doctrine, his habits and the <i>seams</i> between him and his allies, where a small force can obtain a large effect. The Byzantine book shows the same idea at the scale of an empire that survived a thousand years by dividing its enemies rather than destroying them. The Seams page lists every such triangle the atlas can see. They are computed from hand-written postures of confidence A or B only. Under the seams sit two lighter tiers. The <b>hedger</b> is allied with one of two rivals and a partner of the other: it has chosen a side and keeps the door open, insurance against both outcomes. The <b>buffer</b> is the partner of both and the ally of neither, the state that belongs to neither and deals with both, which Rome kept on purpose between itself and its enemies; the buffer profits from the quarrel until it is forced to choose.

8. <p>A <b>client</b> is an actor that leans on another more than it is leaned on: it grades the other higher than the other grades it. Luttwak's name for the arrangement is the <b>client system</b> of the early Roman empire, described in <i>The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire</i>: the hegemonic empire of the Julio-Claudians ruled through client kings whose loyalty exceeded Rome's commitment to them, which is why it was cheap. The Clients page measures the <b>lean</b> of every pair the atlas has read from both sides, in steps on the posture scale; the higher-grading side is the client, the other the patron. A client bound by kin or affinity is loyal; one bound by nothing, or by a grievance, is bought, and holds as long as the patron pays. The tepid ally, graded "ally" by its patron and answering "reluctant ally", is the costly kind, the one Rome subsidised. In the ledger under the map the lean column shows, for each counterpart, who leans on whom.

9. <p>A <b>gathering</b> is the coalition that an actor's weight summons. The frame is <i>The Rise of China vs. the Logic of Strategy</i>: a power that grows or presses provokes, by the logic of strategy alone, the convergence of everyone it threatens, whether or not they speak to each other, and the great state, absorbed in its own scale, is the last to see it. Byzantium made a method of the same law from the other side, recruiting the enemies of its enemies. The Gatherings page lists, for every actor with at least five declared rivals, who has gathered against it, and measures how far the gathering has become a coalition: formed when most of its members are already allied with each other, waiting when they are not, which is the coalition the logic is still summoning. Each gathering is weighed, as a share of the world's economy and armed spending, against the weight of its target; and for each the page says which members the target itself does not read as hostile, the part of the gathering it has not seen. Under the gatherings sits the <b>encirclement</b>: for every actor, the share of its neighbours that stand against it, since the neighbours are the first the logic summons. In each actor's dossier the <b>convergences</b> are the other side of the same reading: the actors that share one of its rivals and are not yet its allies, the tables it has not sat at.

10. <p>Below grand strategy in Luttwak's ladder of levels sits the <b>theatre</b>, where geography and several actors' forces add up and where strategy is actually conducted. The atlas keeps a hand list of ${THEATRES.length} theatres, from the Western Balkans to the Southern Cone, and computes for each a <b>heat</b> from what it has read between the members: declared hostility, seams, feuds, claimed territory and movements with stakes, divided by the number of possible pairs so that a small dense theatre is not outweighed by a large one. The Theatres page has its own map, lit by heat, and the list of what makes each theatre hot. A theatre whose members have few cards reads cold for lack of data, not for peace.

11. <p class="lede">The level below grand strategy in Luttwak's ladder: the theatre, where geography and several actors' forces add up, and where the readings on these pages become acute. There are ${THEATRES.length} theatres, kept by hand; an actor may sit in more than one. The heat of a theatre is computed from what the atlas has read between its members: declared hostility, seams, feuds, claimed territory and movements with stakes.

12. <p>In 1990 Luttwak gave a name to the form rivalry would take once the great wars were over: <b>geo-economics</b>, the logic of conflict in the grammar of commerce, capital in place of firepower, market penetration in place of garrisons. The atlas keeps the material dependencies between actors with their numbers: energy, trade, debt, investment, bases, arms, remittances. The Levers page lists every such dependency between two actors of which one reads the other as rival or adversary, the handle one side holds over the other, or that both hold. It is a first cut; the direction of each lever is in the row's summary, not yet in a field of its own.

13. <p class="lede">The logic of conflict in the grammar of commerce. In 1990 Luttwak wrote that with the great wars over, states would carry their rivalries into the economy, with capital for firepower, market penetration for garrisons and research for armament: <i>geo-economics</i>. The atlas keeps ${STATS && STATS.relations ? Number(STATS.relations.dependency_with_number).toLocaleString("en-GB") : "many"} dependency rows with a number in them: energy, trade, debt, investment, bases, arms and remittances. This page is the first cut of the lever: for every pair of actors of which at least one reads the other as rival or adversary, what still binds them materially. The gas a bloc buys from the power it sanctions, the market a rival cannot do without, the ground an enemy holds. Heaviest pairs first (the two weights multiplied, so a pair counts when both sides matter); one line per lever, from the card that wrote it, cut to its first sentence: the full row is in the dossier.

14. <p>A seam needs three legs: A hostile to X (rival or adversary), A allied to Y, X allied to Y (ally or reluctant ally). Every leg must be a written reading of confidence A or B, from either side of the leg. A treaty membership alone never counts, or every state in both NATO and the EU would be pulled between Cyprus and Turkey; provisional reciprocals never count; a de facto alignment counts as an allied leg. A <b>hedger</b> needs the same hostile leg, one allied leg (ally, reluctant ally or de facto) and one partner leg (partner or reluctant partner, written A or B from either side); a <b>buffer</b> needs two partner legs and no allied one. A triple already counted as a seam is neither; organisations never hedge or buffer. ${SEAMS.length} seams, ${HEDGES.length} hedgers and ${BUFFERS.length} buffers so far, from ${fullActors.length} cards; the lists grow by themselves as cards are written on both sides of a rivalry.

15. <p>Three conditions, all of them in the build: both readings must be hand-written at confidence A or B (provisional reciprocals and treaty-floor stances never count, so a pair appears only when both cards exist); at least one side must be an alliance (ally or reluctant ally), because a lean between two partners is a mood, not a client system; and the lean must be positive, so each pair appears once, from the client's side. Two further guards: two allies who differ only in warmth, one reading ally and the other reluctant ally, are not a client system but a <b>tepid alliance</b>, listed apart below; and a patron must carry at least half the weight of its client, weight being measured for now by how many cards read each actor, so that a great power never appears as the client of a small one. The last column reads the client's bond: <b>loyal</b> if kin or affinity, <b>bought</b> if none or grievance, since a bought client holds only while the patron pays.

17. <p class="lede">Luttwak's second rule for Byzantium, in <i>The Grand Strategy of the Byzantine Empire</i>: gather intelligence on the other and his mentality, and monitor his movements continuously. Constantinople kept an office for it, the Bureau of Barbarians, which read every envoy and every rumour from beyond the border. This page is the atlas's monitor: the events that GDELT codes from the world's news in the last seven days are counted per country and set against the country's own four-week average. A country is <b>surging</b> when it makes a multiple of its normal, whatever its size; it is <b>burning</b> when the conflict in its news runs above its normal. The loud, who make the most news every week, are listed apart so they do not hide the rest.

18. <p><b>Surging</b> is the week's events divided by the country's own mean week over the four weeks before, smoothed so that very small counts do not explode; only countries with at least 150 events in the week are ranked. <b>Burning</b> takes the conflict events alone (verbal and material conflict, GDELT's classes 3 and 4), divides them by the country's normal conflict, and multiplies by the share of conflict in the week's news; same floor, and a share of at least 30%. <b>Loud</b> is the plain count of articles. Each country is set against itself, so the United States, which makes more than a quarter of the world's coded news, is loud but seldom surging.

19. <p>What it is not: it measures the attention of the media GDELT reads, mostly in English and other major languages, not what happened. A hurricane and a coup read alike until the conflict share tells them apart; the francophone Sahel and the Pacific read quiet for lack of coverage; a surge may be a summit or a scandal. It is a pointer, kept apart from the cards: each update starts its searches from the surging and the burning, and nothing enters a card from here without a source read by a person. Nothing on this page is a reading of the atlas.

21. <p>Under the postures sit the relation rows, in four layers: <b>objectives</b> (what an actor wants from another), <b>alignments</b> (treaties, memberships, recognitions), <b>dependencies</b> (energy, trade, debt, arms, remittances, with numbers) and <b>actions</b> (dated events: votes, sanctions, visits, exercises). The datasets are the floor: memberships from Wikidata and official lists, recognition tables from Wikipedia with their citations, every recorded vote of the UN General Assembly since 2022 from the UN Digital Library. The cards are the reading on top of that floor. A lighter floor still is the <b>Watch</b>: the events GDELT codes from the world's news in the last seven days are counted per country and set against the country's own four-week average, Luttwak's Byzantine rule of monitoring the other continuously; attention, not truth, kept apart from the cards, and a pointer for where the next update should look.

22. <p>The data lives in plain files, one per actor, separate from the site, and is versioned in the open: the whole history of every grade is in the repository. Cards are researched and drafted by language-model agents working from a shared brief and the atlas's own rules, then reread by a stronger model that checks objectives, paradoxes and every posture grade against the sources and against the neighbouring cards; the editor samples the result. A new card's grades retroact on the cards already written as provisional reciprocals, flagged as such until written from the other side. Nothing is embedded in the site: it renders the data as it stands on the day of the last build, and the stamp in the top bar says which day that was. The <a href="#/data">Data</a> page counts all of it: actors, readings, rows, sources by tier, the datasets and when they were taken, what the build derives, and what it has cost.

23. <p>There is no server. The site is static files on GitHub Pages: one HTML page, one stylesheet, one script, the compiled JSON, one small bundle per actor, the world outline (Natural Earth 110m, with Kosovo added) and a map from country ids to flag codes. The browser loads the main JSON once and computes the map and the intelligence pages from it; an actor's bundle, with its card texts, notes and sources, is fetched only when you open its dossier or its point of view. Routing is in the URL hash, so every view has an address and the back button works. The outside dependencies are D3 for the maps (from cdnjs), three typefaces from Google Fonts, the flag images from flagcdn, the portrait of Luttwak from Wikimedia Commons, and, on the Data page, one request to the GitHub API for the date of the last commit; each of those hosts sees the visitor's address, as any host does. No analytics, no cookies, no account. The only thing kept in the browser is the language you choose, and nothing you do on the site is sent to anyone.

24. <p>Everything is versioned in git, in the open: the whole history of every grade is in the repository, and a change to the ontology is made only with a journal entry. The atlas is updated on the editor's call, never on a calendar; so far he has called it once a week. When he calls, the datasets are refreshed first (they cost nothing), then the news heat from GDELT and the dates the cards said to watch, then a pass over Wikipedia's daily current-events pages, each event with its news citation, that proposes candidate rows, which he sorts by hand. A card that a major event touches, an election, a change of government, a war, a treaty, is then reread against the outcome: a dated row added, a trend or a grade moved when a source says so, never a rewrite. Between calls, single news items go in as one dated row each.
